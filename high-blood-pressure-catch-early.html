You Can't Feel High Blood Pressure. That's the Trap.

Nearly half of U.S. adults have high blood pressure, most feel nothing, and only about 1 in 4 who have it keep it controlled. Here's how to catch it early and what actually brings it down.

2026-11-02By Dr. Manuel Nunez

You feel fine. Maybe even great. So when the cuff tightens around your arm and the number comes back high, the first thought is usually: the machine's wrong.

Sometimes it is. But high blood pressure usually doesn't make you feel anything - no ache, no warning light. That's exactly what makes it dangerous. It's the pickpocket of health problems: most people don't notice a thing until real damage is done.

Nearly half of U.S. adults have it (about 48%, roughly 120 million people) - meaning a reading of 130/80 or higher, or taking medication for it. Because it rarely announces itself, about 1 in 6 adults who have it don't know. And of everyone who has it, only about 1 in 4 have it under control. Meanwhile, in 2024 it was a primary or contributing cause of more than 680,000 U.S. deaths, largely through heart attack, stroke, kidney damage, and heart failure.

What the numbers mean

A reading has two numbers. The top one (systolic) is the pressure when your heart squeezes. The bottom one (diastolic) is the pressure when your heart rests between beats. Both matter.

Here's how the 2025 guideline from the American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology sorts it:

  • Normal: below 120 and below 80
  • Elevated: 120 to 129 and below 80
  • Stage 1: 130 to 139 or 80 to 89
  • Stage 2: 140 or higher, or 90 or higher

One high reading doesn't mean you have it. A pattern of them is worth acting on.

The most important move: know your number

Because you usually can't feel it, the way to catch high blood pressure early is to measure it. A home cuff is inexpensive and works well - if you use it right. Most people don't. Here's how:

  1. Choose an automatic, upper-arm cuff that's been validated (there's a list at validatebp.org) and that's the right size for your arm. Wrist and finger cuffs are less reliable.
  2. For 30 minutes before: no caffeine, no smoking, no exercise. Empty your bladder.
  3. Sit with your back supported and rest quietly for 5 minutes. No talking, no phone.
  4. Rest your arm on a table at heart level, cuff on bare skin just above the elbow.
  5. Same time each day. Take two readings a minute apart and write them down.

Bring your cuff to a visit about once a year so it can be checked against the office machine.

One high reading isn't an emergency. If a number comes back higher than normal, take it a second time before you read anything into it. But know the two situations that change that:

Call 911 right away

If a reading is over 180 on top or 120 on the bottom AND you have chest pain, shortness of breath, numbness or weakness, vision changes, or trouble speaking, call 911 now. Don't wait to see if it comes down on its own.

High but you feel fine

If a repeat reading is still over 180/120 but you have no symptoms, that's severe hypertension. Contact your clinician promptly - the same day - so they can decide the right next step for you.

What actually brings it down

This is the part I want you to hear, because it's where the power is. You don't need a total life overhaul. Pick one or two of these and do them consistently:

  • Ease up on sodium and lean toward a DASH-style plate: more vegetables, fruit, and whole foods, less processed and salty stuff.
  • Move more. Even a daily walk counts.
  • If you drink, cut back or cut it out.
  • Work toward a healthier weight and find ways to manage stress.

A quick caution on potassium

DASH-style eating is rich in potassium, but ramping up potassium isn't right for everyone - especially if you have kidney disease or take certain blood-pressure medicines. Check with your clinician before you load up on it.

Here's the honest part: lifestyle changes help a lot, and for some people they're enough. But for many others, medication is the right call - and that's not a failure. It's a tool that prevents strokes and heart failure. For most adults the goal is a number under 130/80, reached the safest way for you.

Why bother now? Because catching this early usually beats finding out about it in an ER. An inexpensive cuff and a few steady habits can spare you a hospital stay.
