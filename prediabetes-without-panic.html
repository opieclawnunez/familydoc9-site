Prediabetes without panic

An unexpected A1c result is a reason to make a plan, not a reason to panic.

2026-09-27By Dr. Manuel Nunez

You go in for a routine visit, get your labs back, and there it is: "prediabetes." That's a word that can take over the whole conversation if we let it. So let's slow down and look at what the number is actually telling us.

An A1c is an estimate of your average blood sugar over about three months. A result from 5.7% through 6.4% falls in the prediabetes range. It's above normal, but below the diabetes cutoff of 6.5%. Those cutoffs matter. So does the rest of the story: your health history, other test results, and anything that might make the A1c less reliable. One lab number doesn't get to tell the entire story of your life.

Don't put the result in a drawer

There are two easy ways to react to a number like this. One is the "I'll worry about it later" pile, where lab results go to collect dust. The other is the "I'm never eating bread again" plan, which usually lasts until somebody brings bagels to work. Neither one gives us much to work with.

Better to use the result as a starting point. What does a normal week look like for you? Where could a little more movement fit without turning your schedule upside down? What are you eating and drinking most days? We don't need a perfect week. We need a plan you can still follow when life gets busy.

Pick a starting point that fits

Pick one activity you can actually repeat. A brief walk is a decent place to start for many people. Count your steps, or time the walk you already take. That's your starting number, not a grade. Over the next month, see if you can nudge it up by about 10%. If you walk for ten minutes now, that might mean eleven. Then check in with yourself: How did it feel? Can you keep it going? If not, adjust the plan instead of declaring the whole thing a failure.

Food can work the same way. Look for one regular item that's bringing a lot of added sugar to the party. Maybe it's the sweet tea you have every day, or the donut that comes with your coffee. Pick one, not your entire kitchen. Try a little less sweet tea, or leave one bite of that donut every other day. See whether that feels easy enough to repeat, then decide what to try next. No food needs a dramatic goodbye speech.

The CDC's prevention program aims for 150 minutes a week of activity such as brisk walking. That's a longer-term target, not a test you fail if you can't do it all next Monday. If weight loss makes sense for you, even a modest change can help; the CDC uses 5% to 7% of body weight as one target in its program. It isn't a judgment on your body, and it isn't the only thing worth doing.

Some people do better with a structured plan and support. Ask about a CDC-recognized Diabetes Prevention Program if you want coaching instead of another handout to lose in the car.

Make the follow-up part of the plan

Before you leave, ask what your result means in your case, whether another test is needed, and when to check in again. The answer can vary. Certain conditions, including severe anemia and some blood disorders, can affect how accurate an A1c is, so tell your clinician if that applies to you.

Prediabetes isn't a verdict. It's a chance to catch a pattern while there's still room to change it. Start with one move you can repeat, then see what the next result tells us.
