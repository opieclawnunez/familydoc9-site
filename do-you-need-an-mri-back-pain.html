Do You Really Need an MRI for That Back Pain?

When an MRI helps, when it just scares you, and the red flags that mean don't wait.

2026-10-21By Dr. Manuel Nunez

Somebody tells me every week, "Doc, just order the MRI." Their back went out shoveling, or lifting a box, or sneezing wrong. They want to see the damage. I get it. Seeing it feels like fixing it.

Here's the thing. Most new low back pain gets better on its own, usually within a few weeks. An MRI in the first month or so almost never changes what we do. It just gives us a picture, and the picture can scare you for no reason.

That's the part nobody warns you about. MRIs of people with zero back pain often show bulging discs, "degeneration," little cracks and bumps. Those words sound awful. Plenty of them are the back version of gray hair: common, normal for the age, and not what's hurting. Then somebody reads the report out loud and suddenly you're afraid to bend over, you stop moving, and you get worse.

So what actually helps in the first few weeks? Boring stuff. Keep moving. Short walks, a few times a day. Heat if it feels good. Over-the-counter pain relief if it's safe for you, which is worth a quick call to your doctor or pharmacist if you have kidney, stomach, or blood pressure issues. Stay off the couch. Bed rest makes backs stiffer.

Now, there are times I order the MRI, and I don't wait. Call your doctor soon if you have:

  • New weakness or numbness in a leg or foot
  • Numbness in the groin or inner thighs
  • Trouble controlling your bladder or bowels
  • Fever with back pain
  • Back pain after a fall, a car accident, or a hard hit
  • A history of cancer, or weight loss you can't explain
  • Pain that is severe, getting worse, or wakes you from sleep

The first three can be an emergency. Go to the ER, don't wait for an appointment.

And if the pain hasn't improved after about six weeks, or it keeps coming back, that's the time to come in. We look at your history, check your strength and reflexes, and then decide if imaging makes sense. At that point an MRI can be useful, especially if you're thinking about a spine procedure.

If you do get one, it uses magnets and radio waves, not radiation. Tell the staff about any implants, pacemakers, or metal in your body before you walk in. It's loud, it's tight, and you have to lie still for a while. Ask for earplugs.

Why do I care? Because the best back plan is the one that keeps you walking and out of the ER, and keeps you from signing up for scans, shots, and surgery you didn't need. Move early, know the warning signs, and get seen when it counts.
