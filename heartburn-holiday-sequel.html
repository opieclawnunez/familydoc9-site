Heartburn Doesn't Need a Holiday Sequel

A few changes before you reach for another antacid, and the symptoms you should never brush off.

2026-11-18By Dr. Manuel Nunez

Quick answers

How do I tell heartburn from a heart attack? You cannot always tell by feel. A heart attack can feel like indigestion. Call 911 for chest pressure, squeezing or pain lasting more than a few minutes or that comes and goes, pain in the arm, back, neck or jaw, shortness of breath, or a cold sweat. If you are unsure, get help right away.

What is the easiest first fix for night heartburn? Give dinner some space. Finish eating at least three hours before you lie down. The couch counts. Moving the evening snack earlier beats rebuilding your whole diet overnight.

Do I have to give up coffee, chocolate and spicy food? Not as a blanket rule. Those foods bother some people, not everyone. Notice what reliably bothers you, cut back on that one and see if it helps. You do not need to put every food you enjoy on trial at once.

When does heartburn need a visit? Trouble or pain with swallowing, unexplained weight loss, persistent vomiting, or symptoms that are not improving. Vomiting blood or passing black, tarry stools needs urgent care. Needing an antacid every day is also a reason to call.

Dinner was good. The burning behind your breastbone afterward? Less impressive. Now you're sitting on the couch, negotiating with your stomach like it's an unreasonable relative.

That burning may be heartburn, a symptom of acid reflux: stomach contents coming back up into the tube between your mouth and stomach. You might also notice a sour taste. Occasional reflux happens. Repeated symptoms or complications may mean gastroesophageal reflux disease, or GERD. You don't have to diagnose yourself over the leftovers.

First, don't give chest pain a cute nickname

A heart attack can feel like indigestion. New or unexplained chest discomfort deserves urgent medical attention, not a home antacid experiment.

Call 911 for chest pressure, squeezing, fullness or pain that lasts more than a few minutes or goes away and returns; pain in an arm, back, neck or jaw; shortness of breath; or a cold sweat, nausea or lightheadedness that could signal a heart attack. Symptoms do not all have to arrive together. If you're unsure whether it's heartburn or your heart, get medical help right away.

Give dinner some space

For familiar, occasional heartburn without warning signs, start with the clock. If reflux bothers you at night, finish eating at least three hours before lying down. The couch counts. Calling it 'just resting my eyes' does not change your position.

A practical experiment: move the evening snack earlier rather than trying to rebuild your entire diet overnight. Track when you eat, when you lie down and when the burning starts. Bring that pattern to your appointment.

Find your trigger, not everybody else's

Coffee, alcohol, chocolate, mint, spicy foods, acidic foods and high-fat meals can bother some people. That is not a universal banned-food list. Notice what reliably bothers you, then reduce that item and see whether symptoms improve.

The evidence for individual food restrictions is limited and varies. You do not need to put every enjoyable food on trial at once. If extra weight is contributing, a realistic weight-management plan can help reflux too. No holiday crash diet required.

Medicine is a tool, not a moral failure

An antacid may help mild, occasional symptoms. If you need one every day, have severe symptoms, or keep having trouble despite over-the-counter treatment, talk with your primary care provider. Follow the product label and ask a pharmacist about interactions with your medicines.

Other acid-reducing medicines, including H2 blockers and proton pump inhibitors, have different roles. PPIs can be very effective, especially for healing reflux-related inflammation in the esophagus. They are not automatically 'bad for you,' and lifestyle changes do not replace treatment for everybody. Review the reason, dose and duration with your clinician rather than stopping a prescribed medicine because of a scary headline.

For nighttime symptoms, raising the head of the bed or using a wedge that elevates the upper body may help. Ask your clinician what fits your situation.

Know when the plan needs a person

Arrange a prompt medical assessment for trouble or pain with swallowing, unexplained weight loss, persistent vomiting, or symptoms that are not improving. Vomiting blood or coffee-ground-like material, or passing black, tarry stools, needs urgent medical care. Don't wait for a routine appointment.

A visit does not automatically mean a scope. Your clinician often starts with your symptoms and medical history; warning signs or symptoms that persist despite treatment may call for testing.

Start with one change you can repeat. An earlier evening meal is a reasonable place to begin. If the burning keeps coming back, make the appointment instead of making room for another bottle of antacids. Enjoy dinner. You don't need the sequel.
