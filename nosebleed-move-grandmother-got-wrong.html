The Nosebleed Move Your Grandmother Got Wrong

Lean forward, pinch soft, set a timer - the move grandma got backwards, plus how to keep the next one from starting.

2026-10-26By Dr. Manuel Nunez

Every family has the same nosebleed drill, handed down from somebody's grandmother: tilt your head back, pinch the top of your nose, wait it out. No disrespect to grandmothers. But on nosebleeds, grandma is wrong on both counts.

Almost every nosebleed comes from the front of the septum, the wall between your nostrils, where a little knot of blood vessels sits right under the surface. Dry air dries it out, and then a sneeze, a hard nose blow, or a kid's fingernail cracks it open. Phoenix makes it worse: desert air outside, heaters drying the house at night. This is nosebleed season.

Here's the right move. Give it a full ten to fifteen minutes:

  1. Sit down and lean FORWARD. Not back. Back sends the blood down your throat, which tastes awful and can make you nauseous.
  2. Pinch the SOFT part of your nose, the squishy part just above your nostrils, below the bony bridge, and squeeze hard. You're pressing the little vessel shut.
  3. Hold it for a full 10 to 15 minutes. Set a timer. Don't peek before it goes off; checking at minute three starts the clock over.
  4. Breathe through your mouth. Spit out whatever collects. A cold pack across the bridge of the nose is fine if it feels good, but the squeeze is what does the work. And don't pack the inside of your nose with tissue or gauze.

Most nosebleeds quit with that, at home, no visit needed.

Once it stops, leave it alone for the next 24 hours: no blowing, no picking, no hot drinks or alcohol, no heavy lifting, no lying flat. The clot is new and it is easily offended.

Now the part that prevents the next one, which is what I care about more. Put moisture back in the fight. Run a humidifier in the bedroom at night once the heater comes on. A saline nasal spray a couple of times a day keeps the lining from cracking, and a water-soluble saline gel at bedtime is another good option; the pharmacist can point you to one. Trim your kids' nails. And teach them the lean-forward move now, before a coach somewhere tells them to tilt back.

Easiest way to remember the spray: park it next to the toothbrush. Spray when you brush. A new habit sticks best when you staple it to an old one.

When is a nosebleed not just a nosebleed?

Call the office if they keep coming back, if your child is under 2, if you're getting them with no cold or dry-air explanation, or if your kid bruises easily on top of the nosebleeds. And if you take a blood thinner like warfarin, clopidogrel, or daily aspirin, a nosebleed is worth a same-day call. Do not stop the medicine on your own, though; that's a conversation we have together.

The NHS advises emergency care if the bleeding lasts longer than 10 to 15 minutes even with the pinch done right. Same goes if the bleeding seems like a lot, you're swallowing so much blood it's making you sick, it started after a blow to the head or your nose looks crooked after a hit, or you feel faint, dizzy, or short of breath.

One more thing about the repeat customers: frequent nosebleeds can occasionally travel with high blood pressure or a clotting problem. If yours keep coming back, that's a good reason to come in. A blood pressure check takes two minutes, and sometimes there's one little vessel a doctor can seal so it stops reopening. Either way, it's a better use of a Saturday than the ER waiting room.
