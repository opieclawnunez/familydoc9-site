Fall insurance you can do next to the kitchen counter

Strength and balance practice that fits in a kitchen, built around one sturdy chair.

2026-10-05By Dr. Manuel Nunez

Nobody plans a fall. It's a wet floor, a dark hallway, a dog that picks that exact second to cut in front of you. Most of the time you grab something, wobble, and go on with your day. Sometimes you don't, and that's the one that ends up with an ambulance ride and a long recovery.

I see this in the office all the time. Somebody stops walking as much because their knees complain, then their legs get weaker, then the world feels a little less steady, and then a stumble that used to be nothing turns into something. It's a loop, and the good news is you can interrupt it.

Why I keep bringing up your legs

For adults 65 and older, falls are a big deal. The CDC says more than one in four older adults falls every year, and a broken hip is one of the most serious injuries from it. Falls lead to about 300,000 hip fracture hospitalizations a year, and plenty of people don't get back to living on their own afterward. I'd rather you never meet that statistic.

Strong legs and decent balance are some of the best protection we have. The national activity guidelines for adults 65 and older ask for muscle-strengthening work at least two days a week, plus some balance practice, plus regular walking or other cardio. Two days. That is not a gym membership and a tank top. It's about 10 to 15 minutes, twice a week, to start.

The chair test, and why I like it

Here's the move I'd start with. Sit in a sturdy chair, one that won't scoot across the floor. Cross your arms over your chest if that feels okay, or keep a hand on the counter if it doesn't. Stand up, sit back down slowly, and count how many you can do comfortably. That's your number. It's not a grade.

Do that a couple of days this week. Next week, try to add one more. If you're at five, shoot for six. Slow and steady beats a heroic Monday that leaves you sore until Friday. Once the chair feels easy, we can talk about steps, a light set of weights, or a class near you.

Every week or two, check in with yourself. Is it getting easier? Are you steadier getting out of the car? Tell me about it at your next visit. I like good news.

Pair it with a short checklist

Strength is only part of this. A few other things pull a lot of weight:

  • Ask your clinician or pharmacist to review your medicines, including the over-the-counter ones. Some make you dizzy or sleepy.
  • Get your eyes checked once a year and update your glasses.
  • Ask whether you should be screened for osteoporosis, and whether vitamin D makes sense for you.
  • Walk through your house like a burglar with a flashlight: loose rugs, dark stairs, no grab bar by the tub.

And if you've fallen, tell somebody. CDC data says fewer than half of older adults who fall mention it to their doctor. Falling once makes a second fall more likely, which is exactly why I want to hear about it.

Before you start, and when not to wait

If you have heart disease, dizziness when you stand, a new joint replacement, osteoporosis, or pain that scares you, check with your clinician first about what's safe. That's not a reason to skip it. It's a reason to build a plan that fits you.

If you fall and hit your head, especially if you take a blood thinner, or you can't put weight on a leg after a fall, get checked right away. Chest pain, fainting, or sudden trouble breathing during exercise is an emergency. Call 911.

For everything else, the move is simple: stand up from a chair on purpose, twice this week, and let's see how it goes. Catching a problem early, or building a little strength before you need it, beats a lot of expensive hospital time.
