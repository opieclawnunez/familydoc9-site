I'm a doctor and I'm still starting from the couch

A Day 1 recap, a run-walk plan, and the one decision that gets you out the door.

2026-10-13By Dr. Manuel Nunez

I've told patients to walk, stretch and eat better more times than I can count. Lately I've noticed something annoying: I'm a lot better at saying it than doing it.

I'm 56. I started a new job this fall, and with it came a feeling of wanting to refresh a bunch of things. Exercise was at the top of the list. I hadn't taken it seriously in years. For months I'd been itching for an excuse to get going.

The excuse showed up at work

A coworker, Karina, mentioned running. Within five minutes I decided that was my sign. She forwarded me a Heart Walk coming up on Saturday, November 14 at Goodyear Civic Square, a fundraiser for the American Heart Association, about a month away. A month. Three miles. Perfect.

Before I even left work, I'd registered and told my Instagram followers I'd be training in public and trying to raise some money along the way. Telling people is a cheap trick, and it works. If you want to chip in, my fundraising page is here: https://www2.heart.org/site/TR/HeartWalk/WSA-WesternStatesAffiliate?pg=personal&px=4095190&fr_id=13239

The secret was my sneakers

Here's the thing I learned on Day 1, and it has nothing to do with running. 5:45 in the evening is usually when I sink into the couch. That's the gravitational pull. So I made a rule: I walk in the door, change out of my work clothes, wash my face, and put my sneakers on before I can get comfortable.

That was the whole secret. Once the sneakers were on, the sunset and the cool October air did the rest.

The plan, and how I ignored half of it

My plan was simple: run-walk intervals, three days a week, with rest days in between. I had Instinct, my personal AI assistant, put the regimen together so I'd have a guardrail, and I felt free to deviate when my body said so. Week 1 on paper was eight rounds of one minute of easy jogging and a minute and a half of walking.

I did not follow that. I jogged about 30 minutes and walked the rest, for a total of 3.15 miles in 46 minutes and 40 seconds, burning around 330 calories. That is a lot more than the card said, and I'd tell you not to copy me. The plan exists so you don't turn Week 1 into hero week.

What I did do right was slow down. I told myself to run slower than I thought I needed to. I've started too fast before and paid for it. This time there was never a moment where I wanted to quit, mostly because I wasn't racing anyone.

The pacers

I ran with my dogs. They kept two steps ahead of me, as if to show me the way. Every so often we stopped so they could sniff around and do their business, which was a nice break for me too. Not a bad rest interval, and nobody was timing it.

How I felt an hour later

Great. Mostly because I did what I told myself I would do. I expect my legs to complain tomorrow, so I stretched, I'll sleep well, and I'm going to take it easy. Three runs this week. My brain wants to run every day. That's how people burn out in week two.

If you've got three miles in mind

Here's the plan I'm following, in plain English, for a healthy adult who's mostly been on the couch:

  • Three sessions a week, never back to back.
  • Warm up with five minutes of brisk walking, then do the intervals, then walk five minutes to cool down.
  • Week 1: 8 rounds of 1 minute easy jog, 1.5 minutes walk.
  • Week 2: 6 rounds of 2 minutes jog, 2 minutes walk.
  • Week 3: 5 rounds of 3 minutes jog, 1.5 minutes walk.
  • Week 4: 3 rounds of 6 minutes jog, 2 minutes walk, plus one longer day to rehearse the distance.
  • Keep the pace where you can talk but not sing. If you can't talk, slow down.
  • A hard week? Repeat it. Nobody's grading you.

A few honest notes. A month is faster than the usual beginner plans, so the realistic goal is covering the distance as a mix of running and walking, not necessarily jogging the whole thing. If you have heart or lung problems, joint trouble, dizziness, or you've been inactive for a long time, talk with your own clinician before you start. Stop and get checked for chest pain or pressure, fainting, or unusual shortness of breath. Chest pain or fainting is a 911 call, not a wait-and-see.

The part for the person on the couch

I know the person I'm talking to, because I've been that person. You get home from work. There's no time. There's no energy. It's never going to happen.

It's one decision away. Just put on your sneakers. Change your clothes, put on your sneakers, open the door and walk through it. If you do that much, you've done something you didn't do yesterday. And maybe, just maybe, the momentum pulls you one step further.

Even if you never run three miles or jog for 45 minutes, getting your shoes on and walking out your front door counts. Do that, and see where it takes you.

Every one of those little decisions is a vote for fewer problems later: a stronger heart, better sleep, and a lot fewer unplanned visits to places you'd rather avoid. I'll let you know how the next three weeks go.
