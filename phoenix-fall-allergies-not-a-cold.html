It's Not a Cold. It's October.

That three-week cold is probably ragweed. Here's how to tell, and what actually works.

2026-10-19By Dr. Manuel Nunez

A guy sits down in my office last week and goes, "Doc, I've had this cold for three weeks. I can't shake it."

I ask him a few questions. No fever. No body aches. Sneezing fits, itchy eyes, nose running like a faucet, worst in the morning. I tell him the good news: you don't have a cold. The bad news: you live in Phoenix, and it's October.

Fall in Phoenix is weed season.

People think allergies here are a spring thing. Spring is the trees - mulberry, olive, ash. But fall is the weeds. Ragweed and its cousins love this time of year, and our wind does the delivery for free. Add the dust we're all breathing and no wonder half the Valley is walking around sounding like they caught something that never ends.

Here's how you tell the difference. A cold is a houseguest: shows up uninvited, makes a mess for seven to ten days, maybe gives you a fever and body aches, then leaves. Allergies are a roommate. They move in. They itch - eyes, nose, the roof of your mouth, the one itch you can't scratch in public. The mucus stays clear. And they hang around for weeks, especially in the morning and on windy days.

What actually works, in order.

Start cheap and boring. It works more often than people think.

1. Keep the pollen outside. Windows closed, AC on, and change the filter - it's doing more for you than half the pharmacy shelf. Check the pollen count on your weather app in the morning and plan around it. Yard work on a windy day? Wear a mask. I know. Nobody wants to. Wear it anyway.

2. Rinse it out. A saline nasal rinse after you've been outside flushes the pollen out before it sets up camp. It feels weird exactly once. Then you wonder how you lived without it.

3. Use the medicines right. The daily non-drowsy antihistamines - cetirizine, loratadine, fexofenadine - are all fine. Buy the generic. Same molecule, less money. But the real workhorse is the nasal steroid spray, and this is where everybody goes wrong: they use it once, feel nothing, and quit. These sprays take a few days of daily use to kick in. Aim the nozzle a little outward, away from the middle wall of your nose, and give it a week. And those decongestant sprays that open you up in thirty seconds? Three days, max. After that your nose gets used to them, and now you have two problems.

When to come see me.

Give the stuff above two honest weeks. Still miserable, come in. Also come in sooner if you're wheezing or your asthma is acting up - allergies light that fuse - or if you spike a fever, your face hurts, or everything suddenly moves to one side. That's not allergies anymore. That's a sinus infection, and it's a different animal.

And if this is your life every October, ask about allergy shots. They're the one treatment that retrains your immune system instead of just chasing symptoms. Not for everybody. For the right person, it changes Octobers.

The point isn't to tough it out. Untreated allergies wreck your sleep, set you up for sinus infections, and turn a fifteen-dollar box of generic antihistamine into an urgent care visit. Handle it early, while it's still cheap.
