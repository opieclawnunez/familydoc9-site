The cough that won't quit

A cough that hangs around after a cold deserves a plan. When to call, and what to bring.

2026-10-02By Dr. Manuel Nunez

You had a cold. The cold left. The cough apparently missed the memo.

At first, you give it a few days. Then you try a different cough medicine. Then somebody tells you it must be allergies, or reflux, or that one thing they saw online. Meanwhile, you're clearing your throat through meetings and waking up your partner at night. At some point, we should stop playing cough roulette and figure out what is actually going on.

In adults, a cough lasting more than eight weeks is called a chronic cough. You don't need to wait eight weeks to call your primary care office if you're getting worse, losing sleep, or simply worried. And an eight-week cough does not automatically mean something frightening. It does mean we should look for a cause instead of just reaching for another bottle.

One cough, several possible stories

A cough can come from the nose and sinuses, the airways, or irritation from reflux. Asthma can show up as a cough even when you don't notice a dramatic wheeze. Sometimes a medicine is part of the story, especially an ACE inhibitor used for blood pressure. A recent infection can also leave a cough hanging around. These are possibilities, not a home diagnosis.

That is why the first visit matters. I'll ask when the cough started, whether it is dry or brings anything up, what makes it worse, whether you have nasal symptoms, heartburn, wheezing, or shortness of breath, and what medicines you take. Depending on the history and exam, a chest X-ray or breathing test may help. Not everyone needs the same test, and buying a bigger test first does not always buy a better answer.

Please don't stop a prescribed blood-pressure medicine on your own because it made this list. Bring the name or a photo of the bottle and let's talk through it.

The tiny bit of homework that helps

For the next few days, jot down three things when the cough shows up: when, what you were doing, and what came with it. Nighttime? After a meal? Around dust or smoke? A wheeze, a stuffy nose, a sour taste? Keep it in your phone if that's easiest. Bring the note and your medication list to the visit. That little pattern is often more useful than an aisle of cough drops.

If a treatment is started, make sure you know what change to watch for and when to check back. A plan without follow-up is just a guess with nice handwriting.

When not to wait for a routine appointment

If breathing is suddenly difficult or seriously interferes with talking, call 911 or seek emergency care. If you cough up blood, contact a clinician promptly; large amounts of blood, especially with chest pain, dizziness, or severe breathlessness, need emergency care. A cough with unexplained weight loss, repeated pneumonia, or worsening shortness of breath also deserves timely medical evaluation. This isn't an invitation to visit the ER for every lingering cough. It is a reminder not to wave away the warning signs while we work on the ordinary stuff.

Most of the time, the useful move is simpler: call your primary care office, bring the pattern you noticed, and let the evaluation guide the next step. Catching a manageable problem early is a lot better than letting it turn into a bigger one.
