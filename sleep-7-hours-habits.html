Prevention

You're not lazy. You're just not sleeping.

Seven hours is the goal. Here is how to get closer.

2026-10-14By Dr. Manuel Nunez

Half the people I see for "fatigue" want to talk about their thyroid, their vitamin levels, or whether they need a magic supplement from the internet. Then I ask what time they go to bed. "Oh, around midnight. Then I scroll a little." And what time do they get up? "Five-thirty."

That's not a thyroid problem. That's a math problem.

How much is enough

CDC says adults need at least 7 hours of sleep a night. Not 7 hours in bed, 7 hours of actual sleep. Some people need more. And plenty of us fall short: in CDC's 2022 data, the share of adults not getting enough sleep ranged from about 30 percent in Vermont to 46 percent in Hawaii, and among adults 45 to 64 it was about 39 percent. You're in very large company.

Why do I care? CDC lists the payoffs: getting sick less often, staying at a healthy weight, better mood and less stress, and a lower risk of type 2 diabetes, heart disease, high blood pressure and stroke. It also helps your attention and memory, and it lowers the chance you'll be in a car crash. That's a lot of prevention for something that costs nothing.

Quantity and quality

Seven hours of tossing and turning doesn't count the same. CDC points to signs of poor sleep quality: trouble falling asleep, waking up repeatedly during the night, and feeling sleepy or tired even after what should have been enough sleep. If that last one sounds like you, keep reading, because it's the one that deserves a real conversation.

Small changes that actually help

Here's the boring list, and yes, it works better than it has any right to:

  • Go to bed and get up at the same time every day, weekends included, as best you can.
  • Keep the bedroom quiet, relaxing and cool.
  • Turn off screens at least 30 minutes before bed. The phone can charge in the kitchen.
  • Skip big meals and alcohol close to bedtime.
  • No caffeine in the afternoon or evening.
  • Move your body regularly and eat reasonably well.

Pick one. Not all six. Do it for two weeks and see how you feel.

When it's more than a habit

Some people do everything right and still can't sleep well. CDC says certain sleep disorders can keep you from getting enough quality sleep no matter how hard you try. If you regularly have trouble sleeping, or you're tired all day even after a full night, tell your clinician. They can figure out what's going on, and sometimes that includes a sleep study.

A good way to prep for that visit is a simple sleep diary for a week or two. Write down when you go to bed, when you wake up at night and in the morning, any naps, exercise, alcohol or caffeine, and any medicines you take. It takes two minutes and gives us real information instead of "I'm just tired."

And a safety note: if you're so sleepy that you're nodding off while driving, treat that as urgent and don't drive until you've talked with a clinician. If you have chest pain, trouble breathing or fainting, that's an emergency. Call 911.

For everything else, here's the move: pick a bedtime tonight that gives you seven hours, and set an alarm to go to bed, not just to wake up. Catching a problem early, or just fixing a habit, beats a lot of expensive coffee.
