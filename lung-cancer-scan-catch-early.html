The Once-a-Year Scan That Can Catch Lung Cancer Early

Who actually qualifies for the yearly lung cancer scan, what it feels like, what it costs, and the honest trade-offs - from USPSTF, ACS, and Medicare rules.

2026-10-28By Dr. Manuel Nunez

Here's a lousy way to find out you have lung cancer: a cough that won't quit, chest pain, maybe coughing up blood. So a quick detour before anything else, because this matters more than the rest of the piece: if you're coughing up blood, call your doctor - any amount, even once. And if it's more than a few teaspoons, or it comes with chest pain, dizziness, or fever, get medical help right away. A cough that won't quit or chest pain that keeps showing up also deserves a prompt call, not a wait-until-your-next-physical. That's not screening. That's getting checked, now.

This piece is for people without symptoms who have a substantial smoking history behind them. Because there's a once-a-year scan that can find lung cancer early, before it ever makes you cough - and November, Lung Cancer Awareness Month, is as good a time as any to ask about it.

Who the scan is for

The yearly lung cancer scan has a specific guest list, and it differs a little depending on whose rules you're reading - here's the honest version. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommends it every year if you're 50 to 80, have a smoking history of 20 pack-years or more, and you smoke now or quit within the last 15 years. The American Cancer Society's 2023 guideline matches on age and pack-years but dropped the quit-clock entirely - if you formerly smoked and hit the other numbers, the ACS says you still qualify no matter how long ago you quit. And Medicare pays for it from 50 to 77, for people without symptoms who smoke now or quit within the last 15 years - with an order from your health care provider.

Quick math on pack-years: one pack a day for 20 years, two packs a day for 10, half a pack a day for 40 - it all adds up to 20, and 20 or more is the line.

If you quit years ago, that was the single best thing you ever did for your lungs. It also may not close this conversation - under the ACS guideline it doesn't. So if you're anywhere near these numbers, ask. A short talk with your doctor sorts out exactly where you land.

What the test actually is

It's called a low-dose CT scan - LDCT if you like alphabet soup. You lie on a table, hold your breath for a few seconds at a time, and the machine takes detailed pictures of your lungs. No IV dye, no needles, and it's painless. The whole thing uses up to 90% less radiation than a regular chest CT.

One thing it is not: a chest X-ray. Doctors tried screening with chest X-rays for years, and it never reduced the risk of dying from lung cancer. LDCT is a different animal.

Why it's worth asking about

More Americans die of lung cancer than of any other cancer - men and women both. The most common type often causes no symptoms until it's advanced. That's the whole problem: by the time you feel it, it's late. Screening exists to flip that - find it early, when treatment works best.

And uptake is low. A study using 2022 data found that only about 18% of eligible Americans were up to date with this screening. If you're in the other 82% and you qualify, this November is a fine time to change that.

The honest fine print

No test is perfect, and I'd rather you hear it from me. Most spots the scan finds - more than 95% of lung nodules - are not cancer, but proving that can mean extra scans or follow-ups. Sometimes it finds a slow-growing thing that never would have hurt you. And it's still radiation, even if a low dose. That's why the guidelines aim it at people whose smoking history puts the math strongly in favor of scanning, and why the official recommendation is a shared decision between you and your doctor, not a blanket order.

When does the yearly scan stop? Under the USPSTF recommendation, screening runs to age 80; Medicare's coverage runs to 77. The USPSTF recommends stopping after 15 years without smoking, and Medicare uses that same cutoff for coverage; the ACS guideline does not use a quit-time cutoff. Screening also stops making sense if a health problem would substantially limit your life expectancy or your ability - or willingness - to have curative treatment. That's a judgment call we make together.

Cost, since you're wondering: Medicare Part B covers the yearly scan at no cost to you if your provider accepts assignment, and most insurance plans cover recommended screening. Follow-up tests, if you ever need them, can come with a copay - worth asking your plan about.

If you still smoke

The scan is a seatbelt, not a license. Quitting still beats every machine we have, at any age, and it's never too late to get real benefit from stopping. Do both if you qualify: quit, and get scanned. And here's an easy trick - next time you're booking something routine, like your flu shot or a refill visit, ask about the lung scan in the same conversation. One appointment, two birds.
