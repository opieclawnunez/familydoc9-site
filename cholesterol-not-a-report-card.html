Your Cholesterol Is Not a Report Card

The lab result is a starting point, not a verdict on your character. Get a plan that fits your actual heart risk.

2026-11-25By Dr. Manuel Nunez

Quick answers

Does a high cholesterol number mean I did something wrong? No. Food matters, but so do the genes you inherited and your overall health. The useful question is what the number means for your heart risk and what to do next, not whether you have been good.

Why do I need a test if I feel fine? Because high cholesterol usually does not announce itself. Feeling fine is not a cholesterol test. A blood test can catch a problem before a heart attack does, and that is a much better appointment to have.

What is one change I can actually stick with? Pick one regular source of saturated fat and swap it. Olive oil instead of butter is an easy one. Add something with fiber, like oatmeal or beans, and repeat it. Oatmeal once, then a month of business as usual, is breakfast, not a plan.

Do I have to go on a statin? Not automatically. It depends on your whole risk picture, not one red number. If a statin is recommended, it does not mean your walks and food changes failed. They work on the same team. Do not stop a prescribed medicine on your own. Call us and we adjust the plan.

The number is information. Not a scolding.

You open your lab results. One number is red. Suddenly you're mentally confessing every cheeseburger you've eaten since 1998.

Take a breath. High cholesterol is a health issue, not a personality defect. Food matters. So do inherited traits and your overall health. The useful question isn't whether you've been 'good.' It's what those numbers mean for you, and what to do next.

High cholesterol usually doesn't announce itself. Feeling fine is not a cholesterol test. A blood test can find a problem before a heart attack does. That is a much better appointment to have.

Read the whole picture, not one red flag

A cholesterol panel includes LDL, HDL and triglycerides, along with total cholesterol. LDL is often called the 'bad' cholesterol because too much can contribute to plaque in artery walls. HDL is part of the picture too, but it doesn't make the rest of your risks disappear.

The plan depends on more than total cholesterol. Your care team considers things like age, blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, kidney health, previous heart disease or stroke, and family history. Two people with the same LDL may need different plans.

The 2026 AHA/ACC guideline updates how clinicians estimate heart and stroke risk, including a tool called PREVENT. It also recommends checking lipoprotein(a), usually called Lp(a), at least once in adulthood. This largely inherited risk marker isn't on every routine cholesterol panel. Ask whether yours has been checked, especially if early heart disease runs in your family. Extra tests and scans are tools for particular questions, not a shopping list everybody needs.

Start with a swap you can repeat

You do not need to turn your kitchen into a punishment facility. Start with one regular source of saturated fat and make a useful swap. Try olive oil instead of butter for everyday cooking, beans or fish instead of some fatty meat meals, or lower-fat plain yogurt instead of the full-fat version.

Add foods with fiber, such as oatmeal, beans, vegetables and whole grains. Pick something you already eat often enough for the change to matter. Oatmeal once, followed by a month of business as usual, is breakfast. It is not a treatment plan.

Move regularly in a way your health allows. A short walk you repeat is a reasonable place to start; build toward the usual adult goal of at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week. Movement supports heart health even when the scale is being stubborn. Avoid tobacco, and don't use alcohol as a cholesterol treatment. Too much can raise triglycerides.

A statin is not a detention slip

Sometimes habits alone are not enough. Very high LDL, inherited cholesterol conditions, established cardiovascular disease and other risk factors can make medication important. A statin recommendation does not mean the walks and food changes failed. They do different jobs on the same team.

Ask what benefit is expected for someone with your risk, what side effects to watch for, and what the alternatives are if a medicine doesn't agree with you. Don't stop or change a prescribed medicine on your own. Call your care team so the plan can be adjusted rather than abandoned.

And be careful with the supplement aisle. The new guideline does not recommend dietary supplements for cholesterol management. A bottle saying 'natural' is not the same as evidence that it prevents heart attacks, and supplements can interact with medicines. Bring the list to your visit.

Leave with a plan, not just a printout

Bring your lab results, medicine and supplement list, and what you know about early heart disease in your family. Ask: What is my risk? What is my LDL goal? Which change should I start with? Do I need medicine? When do we recheck, and who contacts me with the result?

The testing schedule depends on your results, risk and treatment. Don't assume that a general screening interval is the right follow-up after an abnormal result or a medication change. Put your own next step on the calendar.

One safety distinction: a cholesterol result is usually a prevention conversation. Possible heart-attack symptoms are not. Chest pressure or discomfort, shortness of breath, or pain in the jaw, back or arms can be warning signs. Call 911 if you think a heart attack may be happening. Don't wait for a routine cholesterol visit.

The goal isn't a perfect report card. It's fewer preventable problems and more ordinary days when your heart doesn't need to be the main event.
