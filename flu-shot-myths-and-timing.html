The flu shot didn't give you the flu. I promise.

What the shot can and can't do, who needs it most, and when to get it.

2026-10-07By Dr. Manuel Nunez

Every October, somebody in my exam room says it. "I got the shot once and was sick for two days. Never again." I get it. Nobody wants to feel lousy on purpose. But let's look at what actually happened, because the story you've been telling yourself might be costing you.

Why you felt rotten

The flu shot is made from killed virus, so it can't give you the flu. What it can do is make your immune system wake up and complain a little. CDC lists the usual suspects: a sore, red or swollen arm, a headache, a low fever, some muscle aches, maybe a bit of nausea or tiredness. Most of it fades on its own within a few days.

Or here's the other possibility. You caught a regular cold, or got exposed to the flu a few days before the shot, and the timing made the shot the villain. Happens constantly. Flu season is also cold season, and the two love to overlap.

What the shot is actually for

Flu is not just "a bad cold." In a typical season, millions of people get it, hundreds of thousands end up in the hospital, and thousands to tens of thousands die from it. CDC estimates that in 2019-2020, the last season before the pandemic, vaccination prevented about 7 million illnesses and 100,000 hospitalizations.

And in seasons when the vaccine is a good match for what's going around, it cuts your risk of having to see a doctor for flu by about 40 to 60 percent. It's not magic. You can still get sick. But a milder flu that keeps you at home beats one that lands you in the ER, and that's often the difference.

CDC recommends a yearly flu shot for everyone 6 months and older, with rare exceptions. It matters most for people at higher risk of complications: adults 65 and older, pregnant women, young kids, and anyone with asthma, diabetes, or heart or lung disease. If that's you, or you live with someone like that, this is one of the easiest pieces of prevention on the calendar.

When to get it

For most people, September or October is the sweet spot, and CDC says ideally by the end of October. If you haven't gotten around to it, you're not too late. Keep going as long as flu is circulating in your community. CDC says most adults, especially those 65 and older, should skip the July and August shot unless getting one later isn't realistic.

You can usually get other recommended vaccines at the same visit, too. Ask your clinician or pharmacist what makes sense for you, and which flu vaccine fits your age and health.

A short checklist for flu season

  • Put the flu shot on your calendar this month. Same visit as another appointment works.
  • Tell whoever gives it if you've ever had a severe reaction to a vaccine. Having an egg allergy doesn't automatically rule you out, by the way. CDC says people with egg allergy can get any flu vaccine that's otherwise right for their age and health.
  • Wash your hands, and stay home when you're sick. Boring, effective.
  • Keep a day or two of soup, fluids, and a thermometer in the house before you need them.
  • If you have a chronic condition, ask what to do if you start feeling sick, and how fast to call.

When not to wait it out

Flu can lead to pneumonia and can make asthma, heart disease, and other conditions worse. Call 911 or get emergency care for trouble breathing or shortness of breath, chest pain or pressure, or persistent pain or pressure in the abdomen. Also don't wait on a fever or cough that gets better and then comes roaring back, or on a chronic condition that suddenly gets worse. After the shot itself, a high fever or signs of a severe allergic reaction, like hives, swelling of the face or throat, or trouble breathing, need medical attention right away.

For everything else, the move is simple: pick a day this month and get the shot. A sore arm for a day or two beats a week flat on the couch, and a lot less than a hospital stay.
